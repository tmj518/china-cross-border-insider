## What happened

The European Commission imposed provisional safeguard measures on imports of grain-oriented electrical steel on 18 September 2026. The instrument is a safeguard investigation launched in March 2026, after the Commission analysed input received from interested parties. The provisional measures take the form of tariff-rate quotas and price thresholds.

A tariff-rate quota allows a set volume of imports at the existing duty rate. Once that volume is exhausted, imports face an additional duty. The price threshold works differently: imports entering below a set price trigger the additional duty, while imports above the threshold pass through at the standard rate.

The investigation timeline is fixed by regulation. A safeguard investigation must normally be completed within nine months from launch. That puts the normal deadline at December 2026. In exceptional circumstances, the period extends to 11 months, which would push the deadline to February 2027.

> **Tariff-rate quota (TRQ)** — a two-tier import regime. A specified volume of goods enters at the normal duty rate. Once that volume is filled, any further imports in the same period pay a higher, often prohibitive, duty. The quota volume and the over-quota duty are both set in the measure.

The definitive measures require a vote. At least 15 EU Member States representing 65% of the EU's population must vote in favour of the Commission's proposal for the definitive safeguard to take effect. That vote happens in the EU Safeguard Committee, and it is the gate between the provisional regime and the permanent one.

## Why now

The investigation opened in March 2026. The Commission spent the following months collecting input from interested parties — producers, importers, and downstream users. The provisional measures landed on 18 September 2026, roughly six months into the investigation.

The timing matters for two reasons. First, the provisional regime is now in force, so every shipment of GOES entering the EU is subject to the TRQ and price-threshold rules. Second, the clock to definitive measures is short. With a normal nine-month completion period, the Commission must conclude by December 2026. The vote threshold is the political constraint. The Commission's proposal for definitive measures needs 15 Member States representing 65% of the EU's population. ## So what

The decision for an importer is whether to secure GOES supply now or wait for the definitive regime. The provisional measures are in force today. The definitive measures, if adopted, will replace them — and the definitive regime can be stricter, looser, or identical.

The cost arithmetic starts with the TRQ. If your shipment clears within the quota volume, you pay the standard duty. If the quota is exhausted when your goods arrive, you pay the over-quota duty on the full value of the shipment. The price threshold adds a second layer: even within the quota, imports priced below the threshold trigger the additional duty.

Consider a shipment of 100 tonnes of GOES with a CIF value of EUR 1,200 per tonne. If the quota is exhausted and the over-quota duty is 25%, the additional cost is EUR 30,000. If the price threshold is set at EUR 1,500 per tonne and your contract price is EUR 1,200, the same 25% duty applies — another EUR 30,000. Both mechanisms can hit the same shipment.

## For you

- **Importers with in-transit GOES cargo:** check the quota status before your shipment arrives. If the quota is exhausted, the over-quota duty applies to the full CIF value. File your customs declaration as early as the rules allow to secure the within-quota rate.
- **Importers negotiating new GOES contracts:** price both scenarios. If the quota is open, the standard duty applies. If it is closed, the over-quota duty adds a fixed cost per tonne. Build both into your landed-cost model before signing.
- **Importers sourcing GOES for transformer production:** the price threshold is the second trigger. If your contract price is below the threshold, the additional duty applies even when the quota is open. Negotiate the price above the threshold or factor the duty into your cost.
- **Importers with long-term GOES supply agreements:** review your force majeure and price-adjustment clauses. A definitive safeguard with retroactive effect would apply to goods already cleared. The EU's 2025 record shows retroactive duties are a real risk.
- **Importers considering alternative suppliers:** the safeguard applies to all third-country GOES imports, not one origin. Diversifying the supplier does not avoid the measure. It only changes which origin's quota you draw from.

## FAQ

**When do the provisional GOES safeguard measures take effect?**

The provisional measures were imposed on 18 September 2026. They apply to imports of grain-oriented electrical steel entering the EU from that date. The measures take the form of tariff-rate quotas and price thresholds. Imports within the quota volume and above the price threshold pass through at the standard duty rate. Imports that exhaust the quota or fall below the price threshold face an additional duty. The exact quota volumes and duty rates are set out in the Commission's implementing regulation, which is the binding legal instrument.

**What happens when the investigation concludes?**

The investigation must normally be completed within nine months of its launch in March 2026, which puts the deadline at December 2026. In exceptional circumstances, the period extends to 11 months, pushing the deadline to February 2027. At the conclusion, the Commission proposes definitive measures. The proposal goes to the EU Safeguard Committee, where at least 15 Member States representing 65% of the EU's population must vote in favour. If the vote passes, the definitive measures replace the provisional ones. If it fails, the provisional measures lapse.

**Can the definitive measures apply retroactively to goods already cleared?**

EU trade-defence rules allow provisional measures to be made definitive, and the Commission has applied retroactive duties in recent cases. In 2025, the EU imposed 26 definitive trade-defence measures and 27 provisional anti-dumping duties, and three cases carried retroactive duties. For GOES, the risk is that a definitive safeguard applies to goods that cleared under the provisional regime. Importers should review their contracts for price-adjustment clauses that cover retroactive duty assessments.
