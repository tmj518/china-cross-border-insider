> **Upgrade protocol** — a later instrument negotiated between the parties to an existing free trade agreement that revises or extends parts of it. It is not a new agreement replacing the original; it amends the original. Until the amended text is published and in force, the original agreement's tariff schedule and origin rules are what a customs declaration is assessed against.

The distinction matters for an importer because the two instruments do different jobs. The original China-ASEAN FTA carries the tariff lines and the origin rules that a declaration is checked against today. The upgrade protocol is the instrument that changes some of those terms. A commentary that announces the protocol without publishing its annexes tells a reader that a change is coming and nothing about what the change is.

## So what

The judgement is this: the protocol is a preparation item, not a pricing item. An importer cannot put it into a landed-cost model yet, because a landed-cost model needs a rate and an origin rule, and the commentary supplies neither. What an importer can do is make sure the paperwork that a future preferential claim will rest on is already clean.

The arithmetic of a preference is worth stating in the abstract, because it tells you what is at stake and why the preparation is worth doing before the text lands. A preferential rate changes the duty term of a landed cost. The duty term is the customs value multiplied by the applicable rate. A preference lowers the rate, so it lowers the duty by the difference between the two rates applied to the same customs value. The saving per shipment is therefore the customs value times the rate gap. Neither the customs value nor the rate gap is in the material here, so the formula is where the calculation stops. Writing a number into it would mean assuming a current rate and an upgraded rate, and neither is published.

The same logic runs the other way for the origin rule. A preference is only collected if the goods qualify under the rule in force, and qualification is proved by documentation. If the upgraded protocol tightens or changes an origin rule, a supplier whose records were built for the old rule has to rebuild them. That work has a lead time measured in supplier conversations, not in days. Starting it after the operative text is published means the first shipments under the new terms go out on the old paperwork.

Work one case through. An importer bringing in a component from an ASEAN supplier holds a certificate of origin issued under the current agreement. When the protocol's origin chapter is published, that certificate is assessed against whatever rule the protocol sets. If the rule is unchanged for that product, the certificate stands and nothing happens. If the rule has moved, the certificate is re-examined and the supplier has to re-document. The importer who has already asked the supplier which inputs come from where, and has that answer on file, resolves the question in one exchange. The importer who has not starts the exchange on the day the text is published.

Work a second case. An importer sources the same product from both an ASEAN supplier and a supplier outside ASEAN. The ASEAN line carries a preference; the non-ASEAN line does not. If the protocol widens the preference, the gap between the two lines widens with it, and the sourcing decision tilts further toward ASEAN. If the protocol narrows or re-rules the preference, the gap narrows and the non-ASEAN line becomes relatively more attractive. Either way the decision is made on the published text, and the preparation that pays off is knowing, line by line, which of your products is claimed under the agreement and which is not. An importer who cannot produce that list on request is not ready for the protocol regardless of what it says.

A third case, for the importer who transships. Goods routed through an ASEAN port but manufactured elsewhere do not become ASEAN-origin by passing through. The origin rule decides, and the rule is what the protocol amends. An importer whose supply chain includes a transshipment leg should map, now, which leg the origin claim actually rests on. If the claim rests on a manufacturing step in an ASEAN country, the protocol's rule for that step is the one to watch. If it rests on a port of loading, there is no claim to watch, and the protocol changes nothing for that shipment.

## For you

- **Importers claiming a preference on ASEAN-origin goods:** pull the certificate of origin for every line you claim and confirm the supplier can state, in writing, which inputs come from where. That record is what a changed origin rule will be tested against.
- **Importers sourcing the same product from inside and outside ASEAN:** build the line-by-line list of which products carry a claim and which do not. The protocol's effect on your sourcing tilt is read off that list, and the list takes time to assemble.
- **Importers whose goods transship through an ASEAN port:** map which leg of the route the origin claim actually rests on. A port of loading is not an origin, and the rule the protocol amends is the one that decides.
- **Importers with a landed-cost model:** leave the duty term on the rate in force and add a placeholder line for the protocol. Fill it when the schedule of concessions is published, not before.

## FAQ

**What does the China-ASEAN FTA 3.0 Upgrade Protocol change for an importer?**

The commentary that names the protocol states that it opens new avenues for trade and investment. It does not publish a tariff rate, a schedule of concessions, a rules-of-origin text, an effective date or a product list. So the honest answer to what it changes for an importer is: the direction is stated and the terms are not. An importer should treat the protocol as a preparation item. Audit the origin documentation behind every preferential claim, confirm suppliers can state where inputs come from, and hold the landed-cost model on the rate currently in force. **Can I calculate a landed-cost saving from the protocol yet?**

No. A landed-cost saving from a preference is the customs value multiplied by the gap between the rate in force and the preferential rate. Neither the current rate for a given line nor the upgraded rate is in the material this article was written from, so the calculation stops at the formula. Writing a number into it would mean assuming both rates, and an assumed baseline is not a sourced figure. The same applies to the origin side: a preference is only collected if the goods qualify under the rule in force, and the protocol's rule is not published in the commentary. The formula is the deliverable until the schedule appears.

**When should an importer start preparing, if no date is published?**

Now, because the preparation does not depend on the date. The work is documentary: a list of which products are claimed under the agreement, a certificate of origin on file for each, and a supplier statement of input origin behind each certificate. That work has a lead time set by supplier response times, not by the protocol's calendar. An importer who starts when the operative text is published begins the supplier conversations at the moment the first shipments under the new terms are already moving. An importer who has the records ready resolves the question in one exchange. The anniversary cluster in 2026 is the signal that the instrument is live in public discussion; the records are what an importer controls.
