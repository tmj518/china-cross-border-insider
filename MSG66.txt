research: the ten learnings, the traffic numbers, and a correction that changes the target

Two documents the operator asked for, plus the correction they forced.

对标学习10条.md — the operator's question was which ten things to learn from the
competitors' success. Ranked for this site, each with the practice and its URL, the
mechanism, the concrete equivalent here, effort, and whether it fits one operator with
no team. Four of the ten are things not to copy, which is the more useful half: the team
size narrative is a price anchor for procurement buyers and copying it would damage a
site whose credibility is verification; daily frequency is a capacity promise that
reading source documents and doing arithmetic cannot sustain; and locking indexable
assets behind a paywall is what zeroed the index value of Trivium's 1,177 source pages.
The single highest-value item is giving each of the 195 figure records its own URL,
because the five-part record already exists and only the address is missing.

对标流量估算.md — hard numbers rather than estimates wherever they exist. The best find
is not a traffic tool: Our World in Data's charity publishes an audited annual report,
and page 9 states 168,000 pageviews a day totalling more than 60 million over 2025,
over 100,000 newsletter subscribers, 775,000 social followers, 14 staff and total income
of £4,551,476. I archived the PDF and its extracted text under the ignored _research and
confirmed those figures in the source myself rather than trusting the summary.

It also caught a widely repeated figure that is no longer true: the "close to 100 million
visitors" number lives on a page that now redirects to /about and survives only on a
staging domain, undated, contradicting the report by five times.

Three calibration points, all verified: ChinaTalk states an audience of 75k in its own
August 2026 hiring post and is the closest analogue, one operator growing to five;
Sinocism shows over 427,000 subscribers at $20 a month; Net Interest claims 100,000+.

Trivium's subscriber count is not public anywhere, and its site traffic is the wrong
benchmark because email reading produces no visits, so its 23,000 a month understates
its readership. The study says so instead of substituting an estimate.

盈亏平衡测算.md is corrected. Its threshold compared revenue against $0.87 a month — the
cost of a domain — so it measured solvency, not viability, and every "break-even"
verdict in it was true and meaningless. The real arithmetic: reaching 100 paying
subscribers needs 2,000 to 5,000 email subscribers, which at a 5 per cent capture rate
needs 20,000 to 100,000 cumulative visitors, median case 66,667. At 1,000 monthly
visitors for a year you capture 600 emails and get 12 to 60 paying subscribers. Off by
20 to 67 times, and still off by 20 times at the most optimistic conversion rate.

The old conclusion is struck through where it stands rather than only corrected in a new
section, because this document was used to justify dropping AdSense. That decision
survives on the reason that does not depend on the wrong number: dropping ads is not
cheap, it is directionally right for a site whose KPI is subscribers.

Also verifies every URL in both documents, which the writing agent could not do — it had
no outbound network and said so rather than implying otherwise. My first two attempts at
that check reported 27 and then 17 broken citations and both were my own bug: the
extraction ran into Markdown backticks, then into the closing parenthesis of a Markdown
link. Third pass: 43 URLs, 34 resolve, 7 are bot walls, and one is genuinely dead — the
Ahrefs checker path, which the study had already reported as returning 404.
