China's medical insurance catalogue is revised on a cycle, and innovative drugs are added to it through a negotiation process. For a new arrival, the practical question is narrower: is the specific medicine I need covered where I will live, and what does that mean for my costs? This guide sets out the checks to make, in order. It is not legal, tax or immigration advice, and no figures are given here because none could be verified.

## What the catalogue change means for you

The national medical insurance catalogue lists the medicines that basic medical insurance can reimburse. Drugs are added or removed when the catalogue is revised, and innovative drugs are typically added after a price negotiation between the authorities and the manufacturer.
Being in the national catalogue is not the end of the story. Local implementation, hospital formularies and the type of insurance you hold all affect what you actually pay. Treat the national listing as a starting point for your own checks, not as a promise.

## Check your own insurance position first

Before looking at any drug, confirm what cover you hold. Basic medical insurance for urban employees or residents is the public scheme; commercial and international policies sit on top of it or replace it, and each has its own drug list.
If you are on a commercial or international plan, ask the insurer directly whether the medicine is covered and what pre-authorisation it requires. The national catalogue may be irrelevant to your policy.

## Confirm the drug and its listing

Ask the prescribing doctor for the generic name as well as the brand name, and the exact indication being treated. Catalogue entries are tied to specific indications, so the same drug can be covered for one use and not another.
Then check the current national catalogue for that generic name and indication. The catalogue is published by the national healthcare security administration; ask the hospital's insurance office to confirm the entry rather than relying on a search result.

## Check the local picture where you will live

Provinces and cities implement the national catalogue on their own timetable and with their own rules. A drug listed nationally may not yet be available, or reimbursable, at the hospital near you.
Ask the hospital's medical insurance office whether the drug is on that hospital's formulary, and if not, what the route is to obtain it. Some cities allow reimbursement for drugs bought outside the hospital under defined conditions; ask what those conditions are locally.

## Plan for the gap if it is not covered

If the medicine is not covered for you, ask the doctor about alternatives that are, and ask the insurer what it will pay for. Get any answer in writing where you can.
Keep the prescriptions, receipts and diagnosis records. They are what you will need for any reimbursement claim, and requirements differ between insurers and cities.
