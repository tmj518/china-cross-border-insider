China publishes a monthly consumer price index, and the release is reported as a percentage change. If you are relocating, the release matters only as a signal about the cost of the basket you will actually buy. Treat it as context for your budget, not as a forecast you can rely on.

## What the release actually measures

The CPI tracks the price of a fixed basket of goods and services bought by households. It is a national average, and it is reported as a change against an earlier period.
Your own costs depend on the city, the neighbourhood, your housing type and what you personally buy. A national average does not tell you what a flat in your target district will cost.
The release is published on a schedule by the statistics authority. Check the publication calendar on the official site rather than relying on news summaries.

## Use it as a cross-check, not a budget

Build your relocation budget from quotes you collect yourself: rent listings, school fee schedules, transport costs and utility estimates for your specific address.
Then compare the direction of the CPI release with what your own quotes show. If they point the same way, your budget is probably reasonable. If they diverge, trust your quotes.
Do not convert the headline percentage into an amount for your own household. The basket weights behind the national figure are not your weights.

## What the release does not cover

It does not set visa rules, work permit requirements, tax residency thresholds or social insurance obligations. Those come from immigration and tax authorities, and they change on their own timetable.
It does not determine rent control, lease terms or deposit rules in your city. Those are set locally and by your contract.
Nothing in this guide is legal, tax or immigration advice. Confirm your own obligations with the relevant authority or a qualified professional.

## Where the release fits in your timeline

Read the release before you fix a housing budget, and again before you sign a lease. Between those points, collect your own quotes.
If your employer or school provides a cost-of-living allowance, ask which index and which period it references. The answer may not be the national headline figure.
Keep a copy of the release you relied on, with its publication date, so you can show what you based a decision on.
