## AI Summary

Canada has extended preferential market access with China through 2028, and its tariff remissions on Chinese steel and aluminium run to the same year. The extension is a date, not a rate: the documents reported here give the year the arrangements run to, a 50 percent target increase in Canadian exports to China, and customs growth figures of 14.3 percent in two-way trade and 20.1 percent in China's imports from Canada. For an importer, the value of the extension is that a known expiry date has moved out, which changes the horizon over which a landed-cost model can be built.

## What happened

The reported material is a set of dates and targets, not a schedule of duty rates. What the Canadian government statement, as carried by China Daily, sets out is this:

- Preferential market access between the two countries is extended through **2028**.
- Canadian tariff remissions on Chinese **steel and aluminium** run through **2028**.
- A target of a **50 percent increase** in Canadian exports to China.

The customs figures reported alongside them are:

- China-Canada trade grew **14.3 percent** year on year.
- China's imports from Canada grew **20.1 percent** year on year.
- Canadian beef exports to China are projected at **500 million Canadian dollars** this year, per Jason Hale, deputy minister of agriculture and irrigation for Alberta.

> **Tariff remission** — an arrangement under which a duty that would otherwise be payable on an import is waived or reduced. It is not a change to the tariff line itself. The duty rate in the tariff schedule stays where it is; the remission is what determines whether a given shipment pays it.

That distinction is the whole of the article. A remission running to 2028 tells an importer that the relief has a stated end year. It does not tell them the rate, the volume covered, the goods covered beyond the steel and aluminium description, or the conditions attached. Those are the parts a landed-cost model needs, and they are not in the reported statement.

## Why now

An expiry date is the one input in a landed-cost model that an importer cannot estimate. Duty rates can be looked up. Freight can be quoted. A remission that lapses on a known date is a cliff, and the cost of the cliff is the full duty rate applied from the day after.

Moving that cliff to 2028 does one specific thing: it lengthens the period over which a shipment can be planned on the assumption that the remission is in force. For a steel or aluminium buyer in Canada sourcing from China, that is the difference between a contract that has to be priced for a possible duty and one that can be priced without it, for the years the remission covers.

The 50 percent export target is a different kind of number. It is a target, not an entitlement, and the reported statement gives no end date for it. An importer should read it as a statement of intent by the Canadian government, not as a volume that will appear.

The two customs growth rates are the evidence that goods are already moving. China-Canada trade at 14.3 percent and China's imports from Canada at 20.1 percent are both rising, and the inbound-to-China leg is growing faster than the two-way total. That ordering is what the extension is being built on.

## So what

The arithmetic an importer can do here is a horizon calculation, and it uses only the dates and rates reported.

Take a shipment of Chinese steel or aluminium into Canada. Under the remission, the duty payable is reduced or waived for as long as the remission runs. The extension moves the end of that period to 2028. The cost consequence is the duty that would apply in each year after 2028 if the remission is not renewed, and the value of the extension is the duty avoided in the years it now covers that it previously did not.

| Year | Remission status as reported | Duty carried in the model |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Through 2028 | Remission runs to 2028 | Reduced or waived, per the remission order |
| 2029 onward | Not stated in the reported material | Full duty rate, unless renewed |
| Contract ending inside the remission period | Covered | No post-remission duty priced |
| Contract running past 2028 | Covered to 2028 only | Post-remission duty priced into later years |

The table carries no duty rate because the reported statement gives none. What it carries is the shape of the model: the remission covers the years to 2028, and everything after that is an open question the importer has to price.

Work it through on a hypothetical, using only the reported facts. Suppose a Canadian importer brings in a volume of Chinese aluminium each year and the remission is what keeps the duty off it. Before the extension, the model had to carry a duty from the earlier expiry year forward. After the extension, the model carries no duty through 2028 and carries the full duty from 2029. The extension does not change the duty rate. It changes the number of years in which the rate does not bite.

The second use is contract length. A supply agreement that runs past 2028 has to price the post-remission duty into its later years. An agreement that ends inside the remission period does not. The extension therefore supports longer contracts than the previous expiry date allowed, and that is a negotiating fact an importer can use with a Chinese supplier.

The third use is the growth figures as a benchmark. China-Canada trade grew 14.3 percent and China's imports from Canada grew 20.1 percent. Both are value measures and both are reported without a stated period, so they cannot be placed against a specific quarter or year with confidence. What they do support is a directional read: goods are moving both ways at a rising rate, and the inbound-to-China leg is growing faster than the two-way total. An importer comparing their own book against these rates should note the missing period before drawing a conclusion.

The fourth use is the beef figure. The 500 million Canadian dollars projected for Canadian beef exports to China this year is a projection attributed to an Alberta agriculture official, not a customs outturn. It is useful as an indication of the scale of one agricultural flow under the improved arrangements. It is not a number to build a budget on.

## How confident

**Confirmed.** The Canadian government statement, as reported by China Daily, extends preferential market access through 2028 and runs Canadian tariff remissions on Chinese steel and aluminium through 2028, and sets a 50 percent target increase in Canadian exports to China. General Administration of Customs figures, as reported by China Daily, show China-Canada trade up 14.3 percent year on year and China's imports from Canada up 20.1 percent year on year. Jason Hale, deputy minister of agriculture and irrigation for Alberta, is reported projecting 500 million Canadian dollars of Canadian beef exports to China this year.

**Inferred.** The reported material does not state the duty rates covered by the remission, the volumes or values it applies to, the full list of goods beyond steel and aluminium, the conditions attached, or the exact date in 2028 on which the arrangements end. It does not state the period covered by the 14.3 percent and 20.1 percent growth figures. It does not state whether the remission will be renewed after 2028. The horizon calculation in this article is a method proposed here, not a finding of the statement.

**What would change this.** A remission order that names the duty rates, the goods and the exact end date would replace the horizon calculation with a per-shipment figure. A statement that the remission will not be renewed would make the 2029 duty certain rather than contingent. A customs release giving the period behind the 14.3 percent and 20.1 percent rates would let an importer benchmark their own book against them.

## FAQ

**Does the extension change the duty rate on Chinese steel and aluminium entering Canada?**

No. The reported statement extends the remission through 2028. A remission determines whether a duty is waived or reduced; it does not change the rate in the tariff schedule. The rate stays where it is, and the remission decides whether a given shipment pays it.

**What happens after 2028?**

The reported material does not say. It states the year the arrangements run to and says nothing about renewal. An importer should model the full duty applying from 2029 and treat any renewal as an upside.

**What period do the 14.3 percent and 20.1 percent growth figures cover?**

The source as reported does not state the period. Both are year-on-year growth rates from the General Administration of Customs, but without the comparison period they cannot be matched to a specific quarter or year.

**Is the 50 percent export target a guaranteed volume?**

No. It is a target set by the Canadian government, reported without an end date. It is a statement of intent, not an entitlement or a forecast of goods that will move.
