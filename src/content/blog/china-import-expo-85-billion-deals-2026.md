## What happened

The ninth China International Import Expo (CIIE) concluded on 10 November 2026 in Shanghai. Organizers reported $85.45 billion in intended deals, a record for the event and a 2.3 percent increase over the eighth edition. The figure was reported by Xinhua and carried by China Daily.

The expo attracted 4,142 overseas exhibitors, also a record. More than 480 products made their global, Asian or China debuts at the event. Free exhibition space for least developed countries increased by 17 percent, a measure aimed at widening participation from the poorest economies.

Two other numbers frame the event's scale. China imported more than 90 trillion yuan worth of goods between 2021 and 2025, a five-year total that gives context to the annual expo's role. Separately, China pledged $1.1 million to WTO programs that support developing and least-developed economies.

The next edition is already taking shape. On 6 November 2026, 49 companies and institutions signed up for the tenth CIIE, scheduled for 2027.

## Why now

The ninth CIIE closed on 10 November 2026, and the organizers released the headline figures the same day. The timing matters because the event sits at the end of a year in which China's import growth has been strong: imports rose 23.6 percent in the first ten months of 2026, as covered in [China's imports rose 23.6% in the first ten months of 2026](/blog/china-foreign-trade-first-ten-months-2026-import-growth/).

The expo is an annual event, and the 2.3 percent increase in intended deals is the second consecutive year of growth. The record exhibitor count and the 480-plus debuts show that foreign sellers continue to see the event as a viable channel into the Chinese market.

The early sign-ups for the tenth edition — 49 companies and institutions on 6 November, four days before the ninth edition closed — indicate that exhibitors are committing to next year's event before this year's numbers were even published.

## So what

The $85.45 billion figure is the largest single signal of Chinese import demand published this year. It is not a sales forecast for any individual exporter, but it tells you three things.

First, Chinese buyers are committing to foreign goods at a record pace. The 2.3 percent increase over the previous edition shows that demand is growing, not plateauing. If you sell into China, the expo's result is a reason to treat the market as active.

Second, the event's structure tells you where the demand is concentrated. The 480-plus debuts mean exhibitors are using the expo to launch products specifically for the Chinese market. The 17 percent increase in free space for least developed countries means China is actively widening its supplier base beyond the traditional exporting economies. If you are a supplier from a least developed country, the expo is a cheaper entry point than it was a year ago.

Third, the number is directional, not contractual. Intended deals are not binding purchase orders. The organizers do not publish a conversion rate, so you cannot multiply the $85.45 billion by anything to estimate actual import value. Treat the figure as evidence of buyer intent, not as a pipeline you can sell against.

The five-year import total of 90 trillion yuan between 2021 and 2025 puts the expo in context. The expo is one channel within a much larger import system. If you are deciding whether to exhibit next year, the record numbers from this year's event are the strongest available evidence that the channel works.

For a seller weighing the cost of a booth against the probability of orders, the arithmetic is simple but the inputs are not published. The organizers report the aggregate figure but not the distribution across exhibitors. A record aggregate does not tell you whether the median exhibitor wrote more deals than last year. It tells you only that the total grew.

## FAQ

**What does "intended deals" mean at the CIIE?**

Intended deals are agreements, memoranda of understanding and letters of intent signed during the expo. They represent a buyer's stated intention to purchase goods from an exhibitor. They are not binding purchase contracts, and the organizers do not publish a conversion rate from intended deals to actual imports. The $85.45 billion figure is the sum of all such agreements reported during the event. For an importer, the figure is a measure of buyer sentiment and market activity, not a forecast of actual trade volume. Use it to gauge whether Chinese buyers are active, not to predict your own order book.

**How does the $85.45 billion compare to previous editions?**

The ninth edition's $85.45 billion is a 2.3 percent increase over the eighth edition's total. The eighth edition's exact figure is not stated in the cited sources, only the percentage change. The increase marks a record for the event. The growth is modest but positive, and it follows a year in which China's imports rose 23.6 percent in the first ten months. The expo's growth rate is slower than the overall import growth rate, which suggests the expo is one channel among many rather than the primary driver of import demand.

**Should I exhibit at the tenth CIIE in 2027?**

The evidence from the ninth edition is positive: a record $85.45 billion in intended deals, a record 4,142 overseas exhibitors, and 480-plus product debuts. The 49 companies that signed up on 6 November 2026 for the tenth edition did so before the ninth edition closed, which suggests exhibitors see value in early commitment. The decision depends on your product, your target buyers and your budget. The expo is a large, generalist event; if your product fits a specific niche, a specialized trade show can offer a better return. The record numbers are the strongest available evidence that the event works, but they do not guarantee individual results.
