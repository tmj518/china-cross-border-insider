## AI Summary

The European Commission's 44th Annual Trade Defence Report records 32 new investigations opened in 2025, 26 definitive measures imposed, and provisional anti-dumping duties in 27 cases. At year end, 232 measures stood in place, 220 of them definitive. Three cases were applied retroactively, backdated to before the provisional stage. The report gives enforcement counts, not per-product duty rates. An importer's cost exposure depends on whether their own product line sits inside one of those cases, and on whether their contract passes a backdated duty back to them.

## What happened

The European Commission adopted its 44th Annual Trade Defence Report, summarised in a trade directorate release dated 18 September 2026. The report counts what the Commission did in 2025 rather than what it charged on any single product line.

The counts are these:

The 32 new investigations sit just below the record high set in 2024, according to the same release. The 232 measures in place at the end of 2025 protect around 637,000 manufacturing jobs in the European Union, again per the release.

The number that matters most for a buyer is the smallest one. In three cases the Commission applied measures retroactively, with the measures backdated to before the introduction of provisional measures. That is the mechanism that turns a duty an importer thought started on a known date into a duty that reaches back into cargo already cleared.

> **Retroactive application** — a duty collected on goods that entered before the measure formally took effect. The Commission backdates the measure to a date before the provisional stage, so entries made in that earlier window are assessed at the definitive rate after the fact. The importer who cleared the goods pays the difference, unless the sales contract says otherwise.

The report does not name the three cases in the figures supplied here, and it does not publish a duty rate for any product. It is an enforcement scoreboard: how many investigations opened, how many measures landed, how many are still standing.

## Why now

The report covers calendar 2025 and was summarised on 18 September 2026. The gap matters. A definitive measure imposed in 2025 is already collecting duty in 2026, and a provisional duty imposed in 2025 has either converted to a definitive measure or lapsed by now. The counts describe a stock that is live, not a historical record.

The 32 investigations opened in 2025 are the forward-looking part. Each one is a case that can produce a provisional duty and then a definitive measure. The 27 provisional anti-dumping cases show how often that first stage is used. The three retroactive cases show that the backdating power is exercised, not theoretical.

## So what

The report gives no rate, so the arithmetic has to be built from the importer's own numbers. What the report does give is the probability structure: 27 provisional anti-dumping cases in a single year, and three retroactive applications.

Take a shipment with a customs value of EUR 100,000 and a product line that falls inside one of the 27 provisional cases. The provisional duty is a percentage of that value, set case by case. The report does not supply the percentage, so the importer reads it from the regulation covering their product. At a hypothetical 20% provisional rate the cash outlay is EUR 20,000 on that entry. At 40% it is EUR 40,000. The report tells the importer that 27 such cases existed in 2025; it does not tell them which rate applies to their goods.

Now take the retroactive case. Suppose the definitive rate is set at 30% and the measure is backdated to cover entries made during the provisional window. An importer who cleared EUR 500,000 of goods across that window at the provisional rate of, say, 15% paid EUR 75,000. When the definitive rate of 30% is applied retroactively, the assessment on the same entries is EUR 150,000. The difference is EUR 75,000, collected after the goods have been sold.

That difference is the whole reason the retroactive count belongs in a contract review. A clause that fixes the duty rate at the date of shipment, and puts any later reassessment on the seller, converts an open-ended liability into a known one. A clause that is silent leaves the importer holding it.

The stock figure gives the scale of the regime. 232 measures in place, 220 definitive, means the overwhelming majority of EU trade-defence exposure is at the definitive stage, where the rate is fixed and the collection is routine. The provisional stage, 27 cases in one year, is the volatile part: the rate is set, the case is open, and the outcome is not yet final.

A third worked case shows why the count alone is not enough. An importer with three product lines, one inside a provisional case, one inside a definitive measure and one outside any measure, faces three different cost treatments on the same container. The line inside the provisional case carries an estimated duty that can move at the definitive stage. The line inside the definitive measure carries a fixed rate. The line outside carries none. The report's 232 and 27 tell the importer that all three situations exist in the market; only the product-specific regulation tells them which line is which.

## For you

- **Importers holding in-transit cargo:** check whether your product line sits inside one of the 27 provisional anti-dumping cases before the vessel arrives, and price the provisional rate into the landed cost now rather than at clearance.
- **Importers with goods cleared in the last 12 months:** pull the entry records for any product under an open investigation and confirm whether a registration order or a retroactive application covers those entries, because the assessment arrives after the goods are sold.
- **Importers negotiating new supply contracts:** write a duty-reassessment clause that fixes the rate at the shipment date and assigns any later backdated assessment to the seller, so a retroactive measure does not land on your margin.
- **Importers with a mixed container:** split the customs value by product line and match each line to its own measure status, because one container can carry a provisional line, a definitive line and an unmeasured line at three different cost treatments.

## FAQ

**Does the report tell me the duty rate on my product?**

No. The 44th Annual Trade Defence Report is an enforcement count. It records 32 new investigations, 26 definitive measures, 27 provisional anti-dumping cases and three retroactive applications. It does not publish a rate for any product line. To find the rate that applies to your goods, you read the regulation for the specific case covering your product, which names the rate and the scope. The report tells you how active the regime was in 2025; the case regulation tells you what you pay. Treat the report as a signal to check your product lines, not as a rate schedule.

**What does retroactive application mean for cargo I already cleared?**

Retroactive application means the measure is backdated to a date before the provisional stage. If your entries fall inside that window, the definitive rate is assessed on them after the fact, and you pay the difference between what you paid at clearance and the definitive rate. Three cases in 2025 were handled this way. The exposure is largest for importers who cleared goods during a provisional window and sold them before the definitive rate was set. A contract clause that assigns any later reassessment to the seller is the practical protection.

**How many EU trade-defence measures were in force at the end of 2025?**

The report records 232 trade-defence measures in place at the end of 2025, of which 220 were definitive. The remaining 12 were at an earlier stage. The 232 measures protect around 637,000 manufacturing jobs in the European Union, according to the same release. For an importer, the 220 definitive measures are the fixed part of the regime: the rate is set and collection is routine. The provisional cases, 27 in 2025, are the part where the rate can still move.
