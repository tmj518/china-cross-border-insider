Almost every first order from a new buyer goes wrong in the same place: the
deposit is paid to a company that does not own the machines.

## What a trading company looks like from the outside

A trading company is not a scam. Most of them are legitimate, and some are
genuinely more useful than a small factory. The problem is not the company
type — it is paying factory pricing for a trader's service, and losing the
ability to audit the line that actually makes your product.

The signals are consistent:

| Check | Factory | Trading company |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Business scope on the licence | Manufacturing, processing | Trading, import/export |
| Address | Industrial park, own workshop | Office building, or "Room X, Floor Y" |
| Product range | Narrow and deep | Everything under one roof |
| Quotation style | Per process, tooling quoted separately | Instant, all-inclusive |
| Video call | Will walk the floor | Will show a showroom, or "the line is busy" |

## The verification order

Do these in sequence. Each step is cheap; the last one is the expensive one,
which is why it comes last.

1. **Company name, exactly as registered.** Ask for the full Chinese legal
   name, not the English trade name. English names are unregistered and two
   companies can share one.
2. **Look up the registration.** Cross-check the legal name, unified social
   credit code, registered capital and business scope. If the scope says
   trading, you are talking to a trader.
3. **Ask one narrow technical question.** A factory answers in specifics —
   tolerances, machine models, tooling cost. A trader forwards the question
   and comes back with a price.
4. **Video call, unannounced.** Ask for a live walk-through, not a recorded
   tour. Have them show the production line, the QC station and the loading
   bay in one continuous take.
5. **Pay for a third-party inspection before the balance.** This is the only
   step that costs real money, and it is the only one that verifies what was
   actually produced rather than who you are talking to.

## Where buyers get hurt

- **Paying a deposit before step 2.** The registration lookup is free and
  takes minutes. Skipping it is the most expensive shortcut in sourcing.
- **Trusting a certification PDF.** Certificates are issued per factory and
  per product. Ask for the certificate number and check it against the issuing
  body, not against the supplier's own website.
- **Using a sample as proof of production.** Samples are frequently made by a
  different line than the bulk order. The inspection in step 5 exists because
  of exactly this gap.

## For you

- **First-time importer:** run steps 1–3 on every supplier you shortlist. It
  costs nothing and eliminates most of the field.
- **Buyer with an existing supplier:** re-run step 2 once a year. Ownership,
  scope and addresses change, and your file goes stale silently.
- **Buyer placing a large first order:** budget for the inspection in step 5
  as a line item. It is cheaper than one container of goods that fail spec.

## The point

Supplier verification is not about finding out whether a company is honest.
It is about finding out whether the entity you are paying is the entity that
makes your product — because everything else in the order depends on that
answer being yes.
