## So what

The judgement for importers is directional rather than priced. This material contains no number, no rate, and no deadline. What it contains is a policy commitment that will, over time, change the cost structure of Chinese goods.

The arithmetic works like this. A green transformation raises the cost of high-carbon production inputs: energy, raw materials, and compliance. Those costs pass through to export prices. The size of the pass-through depends on the specific policy instrument, which this explainer does not specify. The direction, however, is clear: Chinese goods that carry a high carbon footprint will become relatively more expensive, and Chinese goods that carry a low carbon footprint will gain a relative advantage.

Consider two scenarios. In the first, China introduces a carbon cost on domestic production. A factory making aluminium with coal-fired electricity faces a higher input cost than a factory making the same aluminium with hydro power. The first factory raises its export price; the second does not. An importer who sources from the first factory absorbs the increase; an importer who sources from the second does not.

In the second scenario, China tightens emissions standards for a specific industry. Factories that cannot meet the standard either close or invest in upgrades. The investment raises their fixed costs, which they spread across output. Export prices rise by the per-unit share of that investment. Again, the direction is clear even though the amount is not.

The practical consequence is that importers should treat green transformation as a cost factor in supplier selection. A supplier with a credible low-carbon production profile is not just a marketing choice; it is a hedge against future carbon-related cost increases. A supplier with a high-carbon profile carries a latent cost risk that will materialise when the policy instruments arrive.

## For you

- **Importers sourcing high-carbon inputs from China:** audit your suppliers' energy sources and emissions profiles now. A supplier using coal-fired power carries a latent cost risk that a carbon policy will convert into a price increase.
- **Importers of finished goods with green credentials:** verify that your supplier's environmental claims are documented. When carbon costs arrive, documented low-carbon production becomes a pricing advantage, and undocumented claims become a compliance risk.
- **Importers planning long-term supply contracts:** build a review clause that allows price adjustment if China introduces a carbon-related cost. A fixed-price contract signed before a carbon policy takes effect leaves the importer absorbing the full increase.
- **Importers comparing Chinese and alternative origins:** include a green-transformation risk factor in the comparison. A slightly higher price from a lower-carbon origin can be cheaper over the contract term than a lower price from a high-carbon origin that faces future cost increases.
