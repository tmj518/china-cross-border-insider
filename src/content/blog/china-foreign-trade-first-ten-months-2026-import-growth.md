## AI Summary

China's General Administration of Customs reported that goods trade reached 44.81 trillion yuan in January-October 2026, up 20 percent year on year. Imports grew 23.6 percent and exports 17.6 percent. The headline pace accelerated from 18.8 percent over the first nine months. For an importer, the figures are a benchmark, not a bill: they tell you how fast inbound demand into China is running so you can compare your own volumes and budget against the national pace.

## What happened

The General Administration of Customs released foreign trade figures for January-October 2026 on 10 November 2026, reported by Xinhua and China Daily. The numbers are these:

- Total goods trade: **44.81 trillion yuan** for the ten months.
- Total imports and exports: **up 20 percent** year on year.
- Imports: **up 23.6 percent** year on year.
- Exports: **up 17.6 percent** year on year.
- The ten-month total-trade pace of 20 percent compares with **18.8 percent** over the first nine months.

The instrument here is the customs release itself. There is no new duty, no new quota and no new licensing rule in these figures. What the release does is set the official measure of how much goods crossed China's border in the period, split between inbound and outbound.

> **Year-on-year growth** — the change in a value compared with the same period one year earlier, expressed as a percentage of that earlier value. A 23.6 percent import growth figure means the value of goods entering China in January-October 2026 was 23.6 percent higher than in January-October 2025. It is a value measure, so it moves with both volume and price.

The gap between the two directions is the part worth reading twice. Imports grew faster than exports over the ten months. That is the opposite of the pattern many buyers assume when they think about China as an export machine, and it is the figure an importer should hold up against their own book.

## Why now

The timing matters for one reason: the ten-month figure is the last full-period reading before the year-end. It covers January through October, so it captures the bulk of the shipping year and lands before the final two months are booked.

What moved is the pace. Total trade growth ran at 18.8 percent over nine months and 20 percent over ten. The ten-month rate is higher than the nine-month rate, which means October's own contribution pulled the cumulative figure up rather than down. The release states the acceleration directly; it does not break out October alone.

The import figure is the one that changed the shape of the story. At 23.6 percent, inbound goods grew faster than the 20 percent total and well ahead of the 17.6 percent export rate. For a foreign supplier selling into China, or an importer moving goods both ways, that ordering is the signal.

## So what

Treat these figures as a ruler, not an invoice. They carry no duty rate and no per-unit cost. What they give you is the national pace against which your own numbers can be placed, and that placement is where the judgement sits.

The arithmetic is a ratio, and it is the only arithmetic the release supports. Take your own year-on-year change in the value of goods you moved into or out of China over January-October 2026, and divide it by the customs rate for the same direction. If your inbound volume grew faster than 23.6 percent, you gained share against the national pace. If it grew slower, you lost share, even if your absolute volume rose. The same test against 17.6 percent applies to outbound volume, and against 20 percent to your combined book.

Work it through on a hypothetical book, using only the published rates. Suppose an importer's combined China trade grew at exactly the national 20 percent. That book is tracking the market and nothing more. Suppose the same importer's inbound leg grew at 23.6 percent while the outbound leg grew at 17.6 percent: the book now mirrors the national split, and the importer has no edge in either direction. The value of the exercise is not the hypothetical. It is that any deviation from these three rates is a fact about your business that the customs release lets you name.

The second use is budgeting. A 20 percent total-trade growth rate is a value measure, so it folds in price as well as volume. An importer building a 2027 budget off a 2026 base should not assume that a 23.6 percent import growth rate means 23.6 percent more physical goods entered China. The release does not separate the two. Budget the value, and treat the volume question as open.

The third use is expectation-setting with suppliers and buyers. When a supplier quotes a price rise and points to "strong Chinese demand," the customs figure is the check. Imports growing at 23.6 percent is real and it is in the release. Whether that specific commodity is part of the growth is not in the release, and the supplier's claim needs its own evidence.

A fourth use is timing. The ten-month figure is the last cumulative reading before the year closes. An importer planning shipments for the final two months of 2026 and the opening of 2027 has, in this release, the most recent official statement of the trend. It is a trend, not a forecast. The release does not project November or December, and neither should a budget built on it.

## FAQ

**What do the January-October 2026 customs figures actually measure?**

They measure the value of goods that crossed China's border in the ten months, split into imports and exports, and compare each with the same period a year earlier. Total goods trade reached 44.81 trillion yuan. Total imports and exports grew 20 percent year on year, imports grew 23.6 percent and exports grew 17.6 percent. Because the figures are values, they move with both the quantity of goods and their prices. The release does not separate the two, so an importer should read the rates as a value benchmark rather than a count of containers or tonnes.

**Why does it matter that imports grew faster than exports?**

It tells an importer which direction of China's trade is running hotter. Imports grew 23.6 percent against exports at 17.6 percent, so inbound goods into China outpaced outbound over the ten months. For a foreign supplier selling into China, that is the more relevant rate to benchmark against. For an importer moving goods both ways, it means the two legs of the book should be measured against different rates. The release gives the national totals only; it does not say which commodities drove the inbound growth.

**How should an importer use these figures in a budget?**

A result above the rate means you gained share; below it means you lost share, even if your absolute volume rose. Do not convert the value rates into volume assumptions, because the release folds price into the same number.
