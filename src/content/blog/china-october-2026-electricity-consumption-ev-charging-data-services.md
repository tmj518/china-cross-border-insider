## What happened

The National Energy Administration, reported by Xinhua on 23 November 2026, published October electricity consumption figures. Power consumption by charging and battery-swapping services reached 18.6 billion kilowatt-hours in October, a year-on-year increase of 59.4 percent. Internet data services consumed 9.9 billion kilowatt-hours in the same month, up 34.7 percent year on year.

The same report covers the January-to-October period. Electricity use by charging and battery-swapping services surged 55.9 percent year on year over those ten months. Internet data services increased by 40.8 percent over the same period.

The China Association of Automobile Manufacturers, also via Xinhua, reported that new energy vehicles accounted for 64.3 percent of new car sales in October 2026.

## Why now

The October figures were released on 23 November 2026, roughly three weeks after the month closed. The timing matters because October is the first full month after the summer peak and before winter heating demand. The 59.4 percent growth in charging electricity is not a seasonal spike; the January-to-October figure of 55.9 percent shows the same pace across the year.

The NEV sales share of 64.3 percent in October explains the charging demand. More than six in ten new cars sold in China in October were electric or plug-in hybrid. Each of those vehicles needs a charging point, and the electricity to run it. The charging infrastructure is being used at a rate that outpaces the vehicle sales growth.

The data-center figure moves separately. Internet data services grew 34.7 percent in October and 40.8 percent across the first ten months. That is a slower but steadier climb, driven by the build-out of cloud and AI infrastructure rather than by vehicle sales.

## So what

The decision for an importer is whether to accelerate or delay sourcing of EV charging equipment and data-center gear. The October figures point one way: accelerate.

The 59.4 percent growth in charging electricity means the installed base of chargers is being used far more intensively than a year ago. More usage means more wear, more replacement demand, and more network expansion. Chinese operators who need to expand capacity will order equipment. Foreign suppliers who can deliver chargers, connectors, cables, and power-management hardware have a window before domestic manufacturers absorb the demand.

The data-center figure of 9.9 billion kilowatt-hours in October, up 34.7 percent, signals the same for servers, cooling systems, and power-distribution equipment. Data-center operators in China are consuming a third more electricity than last October. That consumption is the physical footprint of computing hardware running at full load. The operators that bought that hardware will buy more.

The cost arithmetic is directional, not priced. The figures do not state what a charger costs, what a server costs, or how the demand growth changes lead times. What the figures establish is the direction of demand. When electricity consumption by a category grows at 59.4 percent in a single month, the equipment that consumes that electricity is in demand. Suppliers of that equipment face order books that fill faster than they did a year ago.

For an importer holding inventory or placing orders, the practical question is lead time. A charger ordered today from a Chinese factory competes for production capacity with orders from Chinese operators who are expanding their networks. The October electricity figure tells you those operators are expanding. The same logic applies to data-center gear: the 34.7 percent growth in electricity use means the racks are running, and the operators are planning the next hall.

The alternative reading — that high electricity growth means the market is saturated and demand will slow — does not survive the NEV sales figure. With 64.3 percent of new car sales being electric, the fleet is still growing. Each new vehicle adds charging demand. The charging network is not saturated; it is being built to catch up with a fleet that is already majority-electric.

For importers of charging equipment, the window is the next two to three quarters. The January-to-October growth of 55.9 percent shows the demand has been running at this pace for ten months, not one. That is not a spike; it is the new baseline. Ordering against that baseline means treating the 59.4 percent October figure as the floor, not the ceiling.

For importers of data-center gear, the 40.8 percent ten-month growth is the number to plan against. The October figure of 34.7 percent is slightly below the ten-month average, which suggests the data-center build-out is steady rather than accelerating. That is still a growth market, but the urgency is lower than for charging equipment.

The figures do not state a price for electricity, a cost per charger, or a lead time for either category. Importers who need those numbers must get them from their suppliers. What the figures provide is the demand signal that justifies the sourcing decision.

## FAQ

**Does the 59.4 percent growth mean charger prices will rise?**

The figures do not state a price for chargers or a change in prices. What the figures show is demand: electricity consumption by charging services grew 59.4 percent in October. When demand for a service grows at that pace, the equipment that provides the service is in demand. Prices are set by supply and demand, and the demand side of that equation is now measurable. An importer should ask suppliers for current pricing and lead times rather than assume a price change. The electricity figure is a demand signal, not a price list.

**How does the NEV sales share of 64.3 percent affect charging demand?**

The China Association of Automobile Manufacturers reported that new energy vehicles took 64.3 percent of new car sales in October 2026. Every NEV sold needs charging infrastructure. A fleet that is majority-electric at the point of sale means the charging network must expand to serve it. The 59.4 percent growth in charging electricity is the measured consequence of that fleet growth. The two figures are consistent: more electric cars on the road, more electricity drawn through chargers, more demand for charging equipment.

**Should I delay data-center equipment orders because the October growth was below the ten-month average?**

The October data-center growth of 34.7 percent is below the ten-month average of 40.8 percent. That is a single month, not a trend. The ten-month figure is the more reliable planning number because it smooths monthly variation. A 40.8 percent growth rate over ten months is a steady build-out. Delaying orders against a single month of slightly slower growth would be a decision based on noise. The data-center market is growing; the question is pace, not direction.
