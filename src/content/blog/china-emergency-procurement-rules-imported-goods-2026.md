## AI Summary

China's Interim Measures for Emergency Procurement Management, effective 1 October 2026, let public buyers purchase imported goods without prior approval. Add-on orders are capped at 30% of the original contract value, and prepayment is encouraged at 40% or more. Importers supplying Chinese public buyers under emergency procurement face a ceiling on follow-on orders and a floor on upfront cash. The 30% cap limits how much additional volume a single contract can absorb, while the 40% prepayment recommendation improves cash flow for suppliers who win the initial award.

## What happened

The Ministry of Finance and the Ministry of Emergency Management issued the Interim Measures for Emergency Procurement Management (财库〔2026〕12号) on 14 September 2026, effective 1 October 2026. The measures govern how Chinese public buyers purchase goods, works, and services during emergencies — including imported goods.

The key change: public buyers no longer need prior approval to buy imported goods under emergency procurement. The measures replace the previous approval-based process with a filing and publication regime.

The instrument sets five operational numbers:

| Provision | Number | What it means for a supplier |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Add-on order cap | 30% of original contract amount | A buyer can order additional identical goods from the original supplier, but the total add-on value cannot exceed 30% of the original contract |
| Recommended prepayment | 40% or more of contract value | For contracts with prepayment arrangements, buyers are encouraged to set prepayment at 40% or above |
| Result announcement deadline | 10 working days after supplier selection | The buyer must publish the procurement result within 10 working days of selecting the winning supplier |
| Outer announcement deadline | 5 working days after the emergency ends | If the buyer cannot publish on time, the announcement must appear within 5 working days after the emergency ends |
| Record retention | 15 years minimum | All procurement records must be kept for at least 15 years from the end of the procurement |

The measures also allow competitive procurement to proceed with only two or one qualified suppliers, provided the procurement documents contain no unreasonable clauses. This lowers the competitive threshold and makes it easier for a single foreign supplier to win an emergency contract.

> **Emergency procurement** — a simplified public procurement process Chinese government buyers use when a sudden event requires immediate purchase of goods, works, or services. Under the new interim measures, buyers can purchase imported goods without prior approval, but must publish results and keep records for 15 years.

## Why now

The measures took effect on 1 October 2026, at the start of the fourth quarter. The timing aligns with China's broader push to streamline government procurement during emergencies, following the experience of earlier crisis-response purchasing that required case-by-case approvals.

The two ministries issued the measures jointly, signalling that emergency procurement is a cross-cutting concern — finance for the money, emergency management for the trigger. The publication date of 14 September 2026 gave buyers and suppliers two weeks to prepare before the 1 October effective date.

The 30% add-on cap and the 40% prepayment recommendation are the two numbers that change supplier economics. The cap limits how much a buyer can order from the original supplier without a new procurement process. The prepayment recommendation shifts cash from the buyer to the supplier earlier in the contract.

## So what

For an importer, the question is whether to position imported goods for Chinese public buyers' emergency procurement. The answer depends on the contract size and the likelihood of follow-on orders.

The 30% add-on cap is the ceiling. If a buyer awards a contract worth RMB 1,000,000, the maximum additional order from the same supplier is RMB 300,000. Beyond that, the buyer must run a new procurement process. For an importer, this means the initial contract is the anchor, and the add-on is a bounded upside — not an open-ended expansion.

The 40% prepayment recommendation is the floor on cash. If the contract includes a prepayment arrangement, the buyer is encouraged to pay at least 40% of the contract value upfront. For an importer shipping goods from overseas, this reduces the working capital gap between production, shipping, and final payment.

The arithmetic for a typical emergency procurement contract:

| Contract value (RMB) | 30% add-on cap (RMB) | 40% prepayment (RMB) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 500,000 | 150,000 | 200,000 |
| 1,000,000 | 300,000 | 400,000 |
| 2,000,000 | 600,000 | 800,000 |

The 10-working-day publication deadline matters for transparency. A buyer who selects a supplier must publish the result within 10 working days, or within 5 working days after the emergency ends if the buyer cannot publish on time. For an importer, this means the award becomes public knowledge quickly, and competitors see the contract terms.

The 15-year record retention requirement means every emergency procurement leaves a paper trail. An importer should keep its own copies of all documents, because the buyer's records will exist for 15 years and can be audited.

The two-or-one supplier rule lowers the competitive bar. If only two or one suppliers submit valid bids, the procurement can still proceed. For a foreign supplier, this means a smaller competitive field can still win — but it also means the buyer is not required to find multiple bidders.

For importers deciding whether to pursue emergency procurement contracts, the decision hinges on three factors: the contract size, the likelihood of add-on orders, and the prepayment terms. The 30% cap limits the upside of a single contract. The 40% prepayment improves cash flow. The publication deadlines create transparency. The record retention creates accountability.

## FAQ

**Does the 30% add-on cap apply to imported goods specifically?**

The measures apply to all goods, works, and services purchased under emergency procurement, including imported goods. The 30% cap is stated in the general provisions of the measures: a buyer can purchase additional or supplementary goods from the original supplier, but the total amount of such purchases must not exceed 30% of the original contract amount. There is no separate rule for imported goods. The cap applies to the contract value, not to the origin of the goods. For an importer, this means the same ceiling applies whether the goods are domestic or imported.

**Is the 40% prepayment mandatory or optional?**

The measures say the buyer is "encouraged" (鼓励) to raise the prepayment ratio to 40% or above for contracts with prepayment arrangements. This is a recommendation, not a mandate. A buyer can set a lower prepayment rate, or no prepayment at all, if the contract does not include a prepayment arrangement. For an importer, the practical implication is that the 40% figure is a negotiating benchmark, not a guaranteed term. The importer should request the 40% prepayment in the contract and document it, but the buyer is not legally required to grant it.

**What happens if a buyer exceeds the 30% add-on cap?**

The measures do not specify a penalty for exceeding the 30% cap. The cap is stated as a general principle ("原则上不得超过"), which leaves room for exceptions. If a buyer needs to order more than 30% additional goods, the buyer would need to run a new procurement process rather than extend the original contract. For an importer, this means the 30% cap is the practical ceiling for follow-on orders under a single contract. Any volume beyond that requires a new award, which resets the competitive process and the 30% cap for the new contract.
