## Definition

This article prices the export corridor for Shenzhen-made robots sold into APEC member economies in the first three quarters of 2026, for importers buying robotics hardware from Shenzhen suppliers.

## What happened

Shenzhen Customs puts the total export value of Shenzhen-made robots to APEC member economies at 4.86 billion yuan for the first three quarters of 2026. The same source gives the year-on-year growth as 7.5 times.

Alongside the customs aggregate, Pudu Robotics — a Shenzhen-based robot manufacturer — reports more than 130,000 robots shipped globally in total. That is a company lifetime shipment count, not a quarterly figure and not an APEC-only figure.

The two numbers describe the same corridor from opposite ends. The customs figure is a value aggregate for one city's exports to one bloc over nine months. The company figure is a unit count for one manufacturer across the whole world. Neither converts into the other, because the customs release states no unit count and the company statement states no value.

## Why now

The growth multiple is the reason this is worth reading this month. A corridor that multiplies its export value 7.5 times in nine months is a corridor where supplier capacity, certification capacity and after-sales capacity are all being stretched at once. That is a buyer's problem as much as a seller's: a supplier whose order book has grown that fast is a supplier whose delivery dates and quality documentation are under pressure.

The macro figures published alongside it point the same way. The National Bureau of Statistics reports China's total goods import and export value up 31.2 percent year on year, and high-tech manufacturing value added up 17.6 percent year on year. Robotics sits inside the high-tech manufacturing category, so the 17.6 percent is the pace of the industrial base the robot exporters draw on — a slower rate than the 7.5-times export multiple, which tells you the export surge is not simply the whole sector growing.

## So what

The decision this article answers is whether the Shenzhen robot corridor is deep enough to build a sourcing plan on, and what a buyer should ask for that the aggregate does not supply.

Start with what 4.86 billion yuan does not do. It does not give a price per robot. Dividing it by anything would be an invention: the release states no unit count, so there is no denominator. A buyer who wants a landed cost per unit needs a supplier quotation, a customs classification, a duty rate and a freight quote — four documents, none of which is this figure.

What the figure does do is size the corridor and date it. 4.86 billion yuan of Shenzhen-made robots went to APEC economies in nine months, at 7.5 times the prior year. For a buyer choosing between a Shenzhen supplier and a supplier elsewhere, that is evidence that the Shenzhen export channel — freight forwarders, customs brokers, export documentation — is handling robot shipments at volume and has been for at least a year. A corridor that moves this much value has working procedures. That is worth something even without a unit price.

The Pudu figure adds a second kind of evidence: a Shenzhen manufacturer has shipped more than 130,000 robots globally. That is a track record of serial production and export at scale, and it is the kind of reference a buyer should ask every shortlisted supplier for — lifetime units shipped, and to which countries. Ask for it in writing, because a supplier that cannot state its own shipment history is a supplier whose export paperwork you will be assembling yourself.

The two macro figures set the negotiating context. With total goods trade up 31.2 percent and high-tech manufacturing value added up 17.6 percent, a supplier quoting a price increase has a general-inflation story available. The 17.6 percent is the number to hold them to: if the industrial base is growing value added at 17.6 percent, a price rise far above that needs a specific explanation — a component shortage, a certification cost, a specification change — not a sector-wide one.

Two figures in the same release belong to different questions and should not be mixed into this one. The national average wholesale price of pork at agricultural product markets was 14.90 yuan per kilogram, and China issued 20 billion US dollars of sovereign bonds in Hong Kong on 19 November 2026. Neither bears on robot import cost. They are recorded here because they were published in the same window, and a reader comparing releases should know they are not inputs to a robotics landed cost.

## FAQ

**Does the 4.86 billion yuan figure give a price per robot?**

No. The customs figure is a total export value and the release states no unit count, so there is no denominator to divide by. Any per-robot figure derived from it would be an invention. A unit price comes from a supplier quotation, and a landed cost adds a customs classification, a duty rate and a freight quote.

**What does the 7.5 times growth multiple mean for a buyer?**

It means the value of Shenzhen-made robot exports to APEC economies multiplied 7.5 times against the year-earlier period. It measures value, not volume, so it does not establish that unit shipments grew at the same rate. For a buyer it is evidence of a corridor under rapid expansion, which is also a corridor where supplier capacity and documentation are under pressure.

**Is the Pudu Robotics figure part of the customs total?**

No. More than 130,000 robots shipped globally is a lifetime count for one Shenzhen-based manufacturer across all destinations. The customs figure is a nine-month value aggregate for all Shenzhen-made robot exports to APEC economies. The two are not additive and neither converts into the other.
